## 1. The problem with what exists today

Three sources are telling three different stories about the same person.

**The website** sells a facilitator. "I help teams move from complexity to clarity." "I bring structure to ambiguity." It is well written and it is true, but it describes a posture, not a capability. Roughly forty thousand consultants make this claim. Nothing in it could not be said by someone who has never shipped anything.

**The resume** sells a product design leader with a Microsoft and Oracle pedigree. Reverse chronological, twenty five years deep, weighted toward roles that ended in 2019. Banyan does not appear on it at all. The most consequential work of the last two years is missing from the document whose job is to show recent work.

**The Banyan record** shows a third person entirely. Someone who designs a platform, writes down its data model, derives the database and API from that model, builds the interface, ships it to production on real infrastructure, and shapes the experience layer of three executive reporting products. At portfolio scale, working hands on.

The third person is the one worth selling. The other two are how that person used to be described.

### The specific gap

The market is saturated with strategists who understand AI. It is thin on strategists who ship the system. That is the whole opportunity, and the current materials give it away for free by burying it.

## 2. Core positioning

Everything below derives from this paragraph.

> I build the systems that turn strategy into working software. Most advisors stop at the recommendation. I keep going, through the model, the interface, and the handoff, so the thing that gets agreed on is the thing that gets shipped. I work from a rulebook: the rules of a domain written down once in plain language, with the technical pieces derived from that rather than maintained by hand. It is a discipline, not a product. It is what lets me carry a project through delivery instead of stopping at design and project management.

**The ten second takeaway:** he builds it, not just recommends it.

**The supporting claim:** twenty five years of product strategy and UX is what makes the systems worth building. The experience is the reason the model is right. It is not a separate credential.

### The arc, stated once

There is a version of this that sounds like reinvention. It is not, and the honest telling is more persuasive than the tidy one.

The aptitude for technology was always there. What was missing was the ability to act on it without waiting for someone else to build. That constraint is gone. AI removed the dependency, and more importantly it changed how the work gets thought about. An agent does exactly what it is told, so vague thinking shows up immediately in the output. Stating the job, the standard for a good result, and the governing rules becomes a habit rather than an exercise. That habit moves upstream into strategy and product design. The thinking got more systematic because the tools demanded it.

**Usable version, roughly one hundred words:**

> For most of my career I could see the system and had to hand it to someone else to build. That gap is closed. AI did not just make me faster, it changed how I think. An agent does exactly what you specify, so vague thinking shows up immediately in the output. You learn to state the job, what a good result looks like, and what the rules are, before anything runs. I use agents that way inside strategy work now: to get into the context, pressure test a position, and put real evidence in front of people early enough to build alignment before execution starts.

**Why this belongs in the materials.** It preempts the obvious skepticism. A reader looking at a design career and a production stack will wonder which claim is inflated. Naming the transition, and naming what caused it, resolves that in one paragraph. It also lands directly on what your audience is currently trying to figure out for themselves.

### What this positioning deliberately gives up

- The generalist consulting audience. Someone shopping for a facilitator will not recognize themselves here, and that is the point.
- Pure design leadership roles. The materials will read as oddly technical for a Principal Design Manager posting.
- "Wide net" search traffic. Tight focus costs volume.

**Note on the second one, resolved.** The function itself is becoming more technical. Product strategy and design roles increasingly expect the person to be able to build. So this is less a loss than a bet on where the role is heading, and the materials should read as early to that shift rather than adjacent to it. Full time and fractional roles are in scope, which is handled with a second resume variant rather than by softening the site. See Section 7.

### The self description, in your words

> A full stack product practitioner. Not just capable of wearing several hats at once, but confident delivering the outcome in each one.

Keep this. It is the most efficient sentence available for the range problem, and "full stack" does specific work: for a technical reader it is a familiar term being used precisely, and for an executive it lands as breadth without needing translation. It also preempts the reasonable suspicion that someone spanning strategy, design, and development must be thin in at least one of them.

**Use it carefully.** It is a claim about depth, so it should never appear next to a list. Say it once, then let the proof in Section 4 carry it.

## 3. Voice standard

These rules are already yours. They are recorded here so every draft can be measured against them.

**Do**
- One line anchor opening. No throat clearing.
- Short sentences. Active voice.
- Concrete nouns. Name the thing that got built.
- Plain language for technical ideas. If an executive cannot repeat it back, rewrite it.
- Close with a next step and a named owner.

**Do not**
- Em dashes. Use a period or a comma.
- Filler. "Circling back," "just wanted to," "leverage," "unlock," "at the end of the day."
- Consultant abstractions. "Drive alignment," "surface insights," "operationalize."
- Adjective stacking. "Strategic, hands-on, results-driven" says nothing.
- Claims without evidence attached within two sentences.
- Methodology promotion. The rulebook is described, never sold. It stays high level everywhere.

**The pressure test, applied to every sentence.** If someone asked one follow up question, would the claim hold? Anything that needs a caveat to survive gets cut or narrowed before it ships. Take pride in the work. Do not inflate it. Inflation is the only failure mode here that cannot be recovered from in the room.

**Tone check, in your words:** direct, positive, professional, forward thinking, conversational, aware. Confident without inflation. Warm without softness.

## 4. Proof inventory

Seven pieces of evidence from the last two years. Each is written as problem, action, outcome. These are the raw material for every deliverable. Nothing goes into a bio or a resume that is not traceable to one of these.

### 4.1 The rulebook approach (the through line)
**Problem.** Most drift starts small. A chart computes its own version of a metric. The documentation describes last quarter's schema. Two teams use the same word for different things. Nobody decides to let a system fall out of sync, it happens between decisions.
**Action.** Works from a rulebook. The rules of a domain get written down once, in plain language the person who owns the business meaning can read, and the technical pieces are derived from that rather than maintained by hand.
**Outcome, and this is the honest one.** It made him materially more productive. Previously the responsibility ended at design and project management, with development handed to someone else. Working this way, a project can be carried from concept through delivery by one person. That is the claim. Not that the method is novel.

**Register, and this matters more than the content.** Describe it, do not sell it. Keep it high level everywhere it appears. Two or three sentences, never a section. The point is not the method, the point is what it lets him deliver.

**What to avoid.** Naming transpilers, toolchains, or vendors. Enumerating what the rulebook declares. Any phrasing that reads as promoting a practice or implying he invented it. The moment it sounds like a product, a reader starts testing it, and the interesting part is the delivery outcome rather than the mechanism.

**Line that is safe to keep:** "Consistency comes from derivation rather than discipline." Use it sparingly, and never more than once in any single document. It is a good line and it does not overclaim.

### 4.2 PM Portal, concept through production
**Problem.** A product management program serving more than one hundred operating companies had no home. Content, coaching, assessment, and deliverables lived in scattered places.
**Action.** Conceived, designed, and built the platform end to end. Seven layers: personas, corporate SSO, frontend, API, PostgreSQL, a content and artifact layer, and containerized infrastructure with automated deployment. Content and navigation are data, not code.
**Outcome.** A live production platform serving PMs, operating partners, delivery coordinators, and program leads.
**Note.** This is the single strongest artifact in the record. Sole responsibility, concept to production.

### 4.3 Training program rebuilt as an execution engagement
**Problem.** Inherited a module based curriculum. Companies completed a course and then went back to work unchanged.
**Action.** Co-led a redesign with the program team. Killed the module sequence in favor of a concept library surfaced at the moment of need. Replaced hard gates with facilitator judgment. Capped CEO time at three to four hours.
**Outcome.** Companies now finish with four artifacts they own: a CEO signed product strategy pressure tested against AI disruption, a prioritized and capacity validated roadmap, a customer facing roadmap ready for any QBR, and a ledger tracing every decision back to evidence.
**Line worth keeping:** "You don't take the program and then go build your roadmap. You build your roadmap in the program."

### 4.4 Experience layer across three executive reporting products
**Problem.** Three different audiences needed three different questions answered from the same portfolio. A CFO tracking cloud spend and compliance, an operating partner tracking workshop progress, and a diligence team tracking a deal pipeline do not want the same view with different filters. They want different products. Portfolio Operations was also fed by generated exports, so the numbers described a moment already past.
**Action.** Advised on and implemented the experience layer across all three. Interaction design, information architecture, and how the underlying data comes together and what each product may legitimately show. Rebuilt Portfolio Operations onto a database backed foundation, replacing generated exports with live data and improving performance.

**The three products.**

| Product | Audience | Covers |
|---|---|---|
| PM Workshop | Operating partners, program leads, participants | Operating company maturity, participation, performance |
| Portfolio Operations | Finance and executive | Cloud spend, escrow, compliance |
| Technical Due Diligence | Corporate development and diligence teams | Deal management, time tracking, people assignment |

**Outcome.** Three products, each built for its own audience and its own question, with Portfolio Operations now reporting current state rather than the last export.
**Attribution, precisely.** "Advised on and implemented the experience layer." Not owned. He did not own the estate, the data platform, or the engineering. He shaped and built the experience layer, and personally rebuilt the Portfolio Operations dashboard onto live data.
**Do not say.** "Five reporting surfaces." That was wrong and is corrected here. There are three products.

### 4.5 A project brief that aligned executives and surfaced a process breakdown
**Problem.** The diligence reporting product needed rebuilding, and there was no agreed statement of what was actually wrong with it or what the rebuild was for.
**Action.** Wrote a project brief for executive review. It identified the opportunities, named the current gaps, and stated the problem without proposing a solution. Alignment on the brief is what leads to scoping, and scoping leads to a spec for execution. The brief is the first gate, not a deliverable in itself.
**Outcome.** An executive team aligned on the problem before anyone designed against it. The analysis behind the brief also surfaced a non-trivial breakdown in process: staging and production were serving two different dashboards.
**Register.** The staging and production finding was a byproduct of doing the analysis properly, not a detective story. Write it that way. Overselling it turns a sound process into a boast, and the process is the more interesting claim anyway.
**Why it earns a place.** It is the clearest demonstration of problems before solutions, and of working credibly with an executive audience. The value is the sequence: brief, alignment, scope, spec, execution.

### 4.6 AI agents that scale one expert across a portfolio
**Problem.** Expert product coaching cannot sit inside a hundred companies at once. An AI coach can, but only if it teaches the same program the rest of the engagement runs on. An agent that gives good generic advice while the portal and the workshop point somewhere else does more harm than no agent at all.
**Action.** Advisory. Defined how the AI coach had to align to the program, what it was accountable for teaching, and how it fits the participant's path through the portal. Set the boundary between what the coach handles and what the platform handles, and made sure the two behave as one experience rather than two products sharing a login.
**Outcome.** An on demand coach that reinforces the program instead of competing with it, reached through the portal at the point in the journey where the participant needs it.
**Attribution.** Advisory only. The coach, the skills toolkit, and the maturity model were built by others. The contribution is program alignment and the integration design, and the drafts must not imply authorship of the agents themselves.
**Why it still earns a place.** It demonstrates something the build examples do not: knowing what an AI product is accountable for, and where it stops. That judgment is the scarce part. Plenty of people can stand up an agent. Far fewer can say what it must never do.

### 4.7 Agents used inside the strategy work
**Problem.** Strategy work is limited by how much context one person can absorb and how much evidence they can assemble before a decision has to be made. The usual result is a position argued from partial information, or alignment attempted after execution has already started, which is when it is most expensive.
**Action.** Uses agents as a tool inside the engagement, not as a product. Given the context of a specific problem, an agent can conduct real research, pressure test a position, and assemble the evidence a decision needs. The purpose is to identify problems and opportunities accurately and to build alignment on the strategy before execution begins.

**Worked example, medical device commercialization.** For an FDA cleared medical technology company focused on breast cancer prevention, built an agent that acted as domain expert across the engagement. It supported the design of strategic pilot programs at multi-tiered facilities, helped identify distribution opportunities, and flagged likely friction with insurance carriers early. That work fed directly into the strategy the team aligned on, before any of it went to execution.

**Outcome.** Better problem identification, opportunities surfaced that would not have come up otherwise, and concrete evidence in front of stakeholders early enough for alignment to be cheap.

**Register, and this is the correction.** The emphasis is on how agents are used, not on the fact that he builds them. Do not describe a practice of building agents for everything. Do not lead with the tooling. The claim is that the strategy work is better, not that there are more agents.

**The second order effect, worth including once.** An agent does exactly what it is told, so vague thinking shows up immediately in the output. Declaring the job, the standard, and the rules before anything runs becomes habit, and that habit moves upstream into strategy. This is a judgment claim rather than a capacity claim, which makes it the more valuable of the two.

### 4.8 Startup work, concept to capital
**Problem.** Two early companies with real technology and no product story an outsider could buy.
**Action.** For an FDA cleared medical technology company focused on breast cancer prevention, led a pivot from a multi state Series A to a focused single state validation program and built the seed investor materials, shifting the narrative from detection to prevention. For an AI driven materials discovery company, translated lab technology into a customer facing platform through facilitation and interactive prototyping.
**Outcome.** Investor ready materials and a defined MVP path in both cases. Phase one delivered on the medical device engagement, with the strategy and investment direction in place.
**Naming.** Neither client is named. Describe both by category. The medical technology engagement concluded after phase one due to internal changes on the client side, so the honest and sufficient statement is what was delivered, not how long it ran.

## 5. Message architecture

One spine, four framings. Same evidence, different entry point.

| Audience | What they are actually asking | Lead with | Proof to feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Company and product leadership** | Can this person reduce my risk on a decision I am about to make? | Problems before solutions. The brief that found the invisible failures. | 4.5, 4.3, 4.4 |
| **Other consultants seeking partners** | What can this person do that I cannot? | Range. Strategy sharpened by real evidence, then carried through to something built. | 4.7, 4.2, 4.1 |
| **Executives** | Will I get a number I can trust and a decision I can defend? | The reporting estate and the no-calculation-in-the-interface rule. | 4.4, 4.1, 4.3 |
| **Recruiters and hiring managers** | What is the last two years, and is it real? | Recency and scope. Portfolio scale, named systems, production infrastructure. | 4.2, 4.4, 4.3 |

**The common thread across all four:** he frames the problem before the solution, puts open questions in the room rather than hiding them, and stands behind the calls he makes.

**A framing rule, added after review.** Ownership leads, handoff follows. Never the reverse. "I hand the work back" stated on its own can read as detachment from the outcome, which is the opposite of what is meant. The accurate order is: I own the decisions I make, including the wrong ones, and the work is built so the team can run it without me. Both are true and the first one has to come first. Any draft that mentions handoff without accountability in the same breath is wrong.

## 9. Reference set: what to take, what to leave

You supplied Morville, Covert, Wattenberger, Appleton, Lee, plus the strategist pattern from Biddle, Torres, and Doshi. Offered as inspiration for a pivot away from how you have promoted yourself before, not as a model to copy.

**The correction, recorded plainly.** An earlier version of this section argued the site should be framework led and carry published writing. That was wrong. You have no interest in being known for your writing, and no interest in maintaining a personal brand as an ongoing project. The site represents you and your core strengths, backed by your experience. That is the whole job.

This is the right call, and worth saying why rather than just accepting it. Those sites work because writing is those people's actual product. Morville and Covert sell ideas. Wattenberger and Lee are researchers whose experiments are the output. A site that promises a body of thinking and then goes quiet is worse than one that never promised it. And your time is better spent on client work than on a publishing schedule you did not want.

### What still transfers

The formal qualities, which are the genuinely useful part and are independent of the publishing model.

1. **One sentence at the top stating scope, not job title.** Costs nothing, works immediately.
2. **Show rather than tell.** For them it is essays and experiments. For you it is the systems you built. Same principle, different artifact, and yours is more concrete than theirs.
3. **Minimal chrome.** Plain typography, fast, no template feel. This is a design decision made once, not a commitment maintained forever.
4. **Restraint as a credibility signal.** None of those sites oversell. The confidence is in how little they strain.

### What does not transfer

- Essays, newsletters, a body of published thinking
- A digital garden or anything that looks stale when not updated
- Framework-teaching as the organizing principle of the site
- Any structure that implies more is coming

**The test for every element:** will this look neglected in eighteen months if nothing is added? If yes, it does not belong.

### What replaces the writing

The proof has to come from the work, which means three things carry more weight than they did.

1. **Screenshots of what you built.** Now the single highest value asset on the site. A real production dashboard or platform interface does what an essay was going to do, and does it faster. This raises the stakes on the Banyan permission question considerably. Ask early.
2. **A method statement, written once.** Not an essay and not a framework to be taught. One durable page or section stating how you work: problems before solutions, model before interface, the rules declared once and derived from. It is a description of your practice, which is a permanent fact, so it does not perish. This is the difference between stating a method and publishing thinking about a method.
3. **Evidence written tightly.** Two or three engagements as short problem, action, outcome entries. Not narrative case studies. Not a portfolio gallery. Closer to the format of Section 4 than to anything on a typical consulting site.

### The idea worth considering

The site itself can be the demonstration.

A fast, hand built, deliberately unfussy site is evidence of the claim in a way no description is. Your audience includes people who can tell the difference between a template and something made, and for the ones who cannot, it still reads as considered. It is also the one artifact you can show without anyone's permission.

**This argues for leaving WordPress.** A static site is faster, costs almost nothing to host, has no template signature, and is entirely within your build capability. The counterargument is that the current theme exists and works. Worth deciding at the end of Phase 2, not now.

## 10. Site structure

Agreed. Four surfaces, not twelve. Every one of them is durable, meaning it stays accurate without being tended.

### 1. Home
Does double duty as bio and thesis statement. One positioning sentence at the top, no hero illustration carrying the message. Core strengths stated once. A short **Currently** block, undated, two or three lines on what you are working on. Ends with a way to reach you.

The Currently block is the replacement for a Now page. It gives the same signal that you are active and working on interesting things, with none of the decay. Update it when something changes, not on a schedule.

### 2. How I Work
The method statement. Problems before solutions. Model before interface. Rules declared once and derived from. The interface performs no business calculation.

**Named this deliberately.** "Frameworks" promises a body of work and implies teaching, which is the publishing model Section 9 rejected. "How I Work" states a fact about your practice. Same content, no implied backlog, nothing to maintain.

### 3. Work
Two or three engagements as tight problem, action, outcome entries. Closer to the format of Section 4 than to a narrative case study or a portfolio gallery. Screenshots embedded here if permission allows, which is what makes this page carry weight rather than just read well.

### 4. Resume
A real page, linked prominently from Home. The existing template is a reasonable starting point. Offer a PDF download alongside it, since recruiters and hiring managers will want to forward a file rather than a URL.

Serve **Variant A**, the consulting and advisory version, from the site. Variant B is sent directly for specific roles and does not live online.

### Contact
A section, not a page. Repeated at the bottom of Home and Work. A separate contact page adds a click to the one action you want people to take.

### Deferred: a "Built" page

The idea is right and the page may be the strongest one available, because showing what you built beats describing it and nobody else in your category has this page.

**Why it is not in the structure yet.** The artifacts that would fill it are the portal and the dashboards, which are not yours to publish, and your skills and agents are real but not readily demoable to a stranger. A page with two thin entries invites a click that disappoints, which is worse than not offering it.

**Revisit when:** Banyan permission is settled, or you have two or three public artifacts worth showing. Until then, what you built appears inside the Work entries.

### Structural rules

- No tabs. Content behind a click is content a skimming reader never sees. This is the single biggest failure of the current site.
- Long scroll over deep navigation.
- Typography carries the design. Type, spacing, and reading measure are the work.
- Every page must still be accurate in eighteen months with no edits.
